\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Introduction}

The area of real estate valuation and market assessment is an interdisciplinary area combining economics, data analysis, and AI. This section discusses the historical progression of methods in this field, including both traditional econometric approaches and cutting-edge techniques including gradient boosting machine learning and large, language processing (LLP) models. In addition, we will describe important aspects of previous research in regard to the Singaporean housing sector (the only two-tier housing system globally). We will also outline particular issues and research availability related to this area which the current project aims to resolve.

\section{Traditional Real Estate Valuation: Hedonic Pricing Models}

Rosen's Hedonic Pricing Model provides the basis for property valuation analysis, 
as it stipulates that all aspects of housing (i.e., location, square footage, 
etc.) can be analyzed, and used together, by assigning a ``marginal price'' for 
each feature of a house to create a total value for a home and that these 
prices can be simulated based on the overall ``equilibrium price'' that 
reflects each of these marginals~\cite{rosen1974hedonic}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical strengths.} An important aspect of HPM analysis is its interpretability and that it permits 
    evaluation of the marginal contribution of each feature to price. The choice 
    of features to be used in this proposed project (\texttt{district}, 
    \texttt{property\_age}, \texttt{sqft}, and \texttt{is\_freehold}) are based 
    upon their use as explanatory variables in HPM model development.

    \item \textbf{Limitations.} The classical assumption of HPM analyses is linear additive certainty; however, 
    this is not appropriate for analysing the extent to which price 
    discontinuities occur in Singapore's HDB market (due to the threshold 
    eligibility deadlines to obtain a $60$-year CPF loan and/or a $30$-year 
    mortgage) as a result of these assumptions~\cite{fan2006determinants}. 
    Additionally, HPMs cannot factor in high-dimensional or non-linear spatial 
    interactions without first constructing and developing feature construction.

    \item \textbf{Spatial extensions.} Can~\cite{can1992specification} and Megbolugbe~\cite{can1997spatial} discuss 
    adjusting the HPM framework to include spatial autocorrelation; the similarity 
    of home prices across geographically close properties. If spatial correlation 
    is ignored when estimating the hedonic coefficients, the estimates will be 
    biased. Therefore, it is important to develop a district-level geocode pipeline 
    in this development to serve as a proxy for the spatial stratification (albeit 
    it does not require a complete implementation of a spatial weight matrix).
\end{itemize}

\section{Machine Learning for Automated Valuation}

\subsection{From Regression to Ensemble Methods}

As the limitations of linear HPMs became apparent, there was growing interest in 
exploring non-linear machine learning models~\cite{park2015using}. In their 
study using the Fairfax County, Virginia housing dataset, Park and 
Bae~\cite{park2015using} found that Random Forests and Neural Networks 
outperformed standard OLS regression on visitor MAPE, and that this difference 
was especially pronounced when significant quantities of features existed. They 
established that MAPE was a more informative statistic than $R^2$, and 
recommended that MAPE and $R^2$ be reported side-by-side in order to provide a 
complete picture of the efficacy of a model.

In Singapore, Fan, Ong, and Koh~\cite{fan2006determinants} explored CART-based 
decision trees to examine how private housing price was determined. Using a 
pre-cooling measures housing dataset, they demonstrated that project type and 
location produced the most significant price splits. While the value of their 
findings has been diminished by the end of the post-2010 cooling measures and 
the emergence of more sophisticated ensemble models, their variable importance 
results are consistent with the SHAP analysis in this study; the 
\texttt{district} will consistently appear as the highest magnitude feature 
across all segments.

\subsection{Gradient Boosted Trees: XGBoost and LightGBM}

In their work on scalable regularized gradient boosting for tabular data tasks, 
Chen and Guestrin~\cite{chen2016xgboost} introduced XGBoost as the benchmark 
for such tasks. The use of second-order gradient statistics, row and column 
subsampling, and both L1 and L2 regularization contribute to its ability to 
handle moderate-sized datasets ($n = 277$ to $16{,}728$) on a per-segment 
basis without overfitting.

After the introduction of XGBoost, Ke et al.~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} developed 
LightGBM, which offers higher throughput via a leaf-wise versus a level-wise 
tree growth strategy, along with the use of Gradient-based One-Side Sampling 
(GOSS). Both models will be trained on data associated with each segment in 
this pipeline. While LightGBM produces a slightly faster training performance, 
XGBoost has provided consistently better held-out test set MAPE performance 
across all eight segments, as compared to both Park and Bae~\cite{park2015using} 
for US residential and Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} for private 
properties in Singapore.

\subsection{Automated Valuation Models at Scale}

The study by Park and Bae~\cite{park2015using} found that various machine 
learning techniques, such as decision trees, neural networks, and ensemble 
methods, could yield lower MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) results than 
traditional hedonic regression techniques when predicting the selling price 
of homes using data pertaining to counties throughout the United States. This project corroborates their
finding for the Singapore context, with XGBoost reducing MAPE by 70.3\% relative to the
mean baseline on the Condominium Sale segment.

Oust, Hansen, and Pettrem~\cite{oust2023machine} took a different tack: instead of
choosing between repeat-sales indices and ML predictors, they stacked both, and the
hybrid beat either component alone on out-of-sample price index accuracy.
This project cannot replicate that approach---URA REALIS transacted prices are not
available to us (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations})---but the finding is relevant future
direction: if transaction data can be obtained, augmenting the current XGBoost models
with a repeat-sales index component is a well-motivated next step.

Jaouhari et al.~\cite{jaouhari2024mapping} conducted a systematic review of 89 AVM studies
published between 2010 and 2022, identifying that (i) gradient-boosted trees dominate recent
accuracy benchmarks, (ii) SHAP has become the leading tool for AVM interpretability, and
(iii) multi-source data integration is an emerging research frontier. The combination of multiple machine learning prediction methods in using an 
XGBoost AVM with individual SHAP-based explanations of each predicted value 
creates an AVM that is capable of meeting the three defining trends of this 
AVM development project.

\subsection{Model Interpretability: SHAP}

SHAP, an acronym for SHapley Additive exPlanations, is an algorithm introduced 
by Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} that uses mathematical 
principles found in cooperative gaming theory to provide a theoretical way of 
attributing the predictive accuracy of a model's output to each of the input 
or explanatory features of that model. The axioms true for SHAP values (local 
accuracy, missingness and consistency) conform to the axioms used to develop 
earlier methods for determining feature importance, such as Gini Impurity or 
Permutation Importance; therefore, the SHAP value is a more principled method 
to establish feature importance than previously developed methods. The 
TreeExplainer is the exact implementation of the SHAP algorithm and is used 
for tree-based models. For the purposes of this project, we will use 
TreeExplainer to produce the top five per-prediction SHAP factors that are 
displayed in the user interface for the various models.

From a practical point of view, Baur, Rosenfelder and Lutz~\cite{baur2022automated} 
established that adding textual representations derived from Natural Language 
Processing (NLP) of listing descriptions and combining them with the 
structured attributes of the listings significantly increased the accuracy of 
AVM predictions; thus providing further evidence that unstructured listing 
data can serve as a complementary predictive signal. This analysis supports 
the chat assistant function of this project by using the SHAP attribution 
data from the structured feature set to base their responses on established 
SHAP attribution data enabling non-technical end users to better understand 
how a model's outputs were determined and thus see how their prices were 
established.

\section{Large Language Models in Real Estate and Search}

\subsection{LLMs as Few-Shot Structured Decoders}

Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020language} demonstrated that large autoregressive 
language models can achieve good results on novel tasks through 
\textit{contextual learning with few training iterations} and without gradient 
updates, by training on a small number of labeled examples within the prompts. 
This capability forms the basis of our natural language search pipeline. 
Claude receives eight query-filter pairs with few training iterations and 
converts free-form property queries into structured JSON filters. Initially, 
an informal version without training iterations (without examples) was tested, 
which apparently led to numerous misinterpretations. Therefore, the final 
prompts include contextual examples.

Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024llm} showed that a transformer-based LLM can pull out 
structured fields (like price, living area, ownership details, and contracting 
parties) from free-text real estate purchase agreements with high field-level F1 
scores. This is better than rule-based extractors in certain situations, like 
when clauses are missing or informal language is used. Our assignment is distinctly unlike theirs in that they re-format contractual 
language into structured format whereas we take user query as input and return 
search filter as output; the underlining mechanism is the same however since 
the LLM will create a mapping between the unstructured natural language 
representation and a structured representation without making task-related 
changes.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} introduced a concept of RAG 
(Search-Enhanced Generation). RAG is a framework consisting of a search 
component which searches an external document base to find similar documents 
and match them with an LLM context prior to outputting a generated result. The 
evaluation chat assistant in this project exhibits similar architecture to RAG. 
The search step in this project is deterministic (database query $\to$ list + 
evaluation context), the LLM uses that as context for generating its 
interactive output. Unlike standard RAG where the search method used to find 
documents is approximate, this project uses exact methods (primary key 
search). Consequently, there is no chance for false positives occurring when 
looking for ambiguous documents.

\subsection{Agentic LLM Pipelines}

Yao et al.~\cite{yao2022react} proposed ReAct as a proactive architecture for 
performing multi-stage tasks using alternating reasoning (i.e., path 
inference) and environmental action (i.e., Act). The iterative filtering 
relaxation method found within this project is architecturally equivalent to a 
single-stage ReAct loop. In conclusion, the LLM examines the query (Act), the 
system executes the database query (Observe), if the query returns zero 
documents then the system invokes the next relaxation (Again, Act). This 
design choice (explicit agent fallback versus passive termination) was made to 
prevent errors similar to those documented in the literature for user 
retrieval using conventional web search filters.

\section{The Singapore Housing Market}

\subsection{Dual-Tier Market Structure}

The residential housing market in Singapore is divided into two distinct 
components: public housing, managed by the Housing and Development Board (or 
``HDB''), and private residential housing. HDB provides 
government-subsidized residential, leasehold property to approximately $80\%$ 
of Singapore's population~\cite{hdb2024annual}, which comes with a $99$-year 
lease and is therefore subject to a minimum occupation period (MOP) as well 
as the CPF Board's restrictions on the number of years required before a 
buyer can sell their property once purchased. Tu~\cite{tu2004dynamics} has 
completed an ideal analysis of the private residential housing market in 
Singapore and has confirmed the volatile mean-reversion behaviour of price 
indices within that space. He has also described how the policies imposed by 
HDB on private housing demand affect the housing market.

Sing, Tsai \& Chen~\cite{sing2006price} provided quantitative evidence of the 
pricing relationships between the public sector / HDB resale and private 
sector residential (condominium) housing, illustrating the impact of rising 
private prices on resale prices of public (HDB) units by using the ``Asset 
Progression'' Price Transmission Model. This inter-market dependence motivates the present project's decision to train \textit{separate} models per
property type rather than a single cross-type model: the pricing dynamics of a two-room HDB
flat (government-regulated, narrow price band) are structurally incomparable to those of a
Good Class Bungalow (privately negotiated, $\geq$ S\$10M).

\subsection{Machine Learning on Singapore Property Data}

Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} applied both econometric and ML methods to Singapore
private property transaction data from URA REALIS, finding that Random Forest and XGBoost
outperform OLS hedonic regression on held-out MAPE. However, their work has three limitations
that the present project addresses: (i) they use a single-source dataset (URA REALIS private
transactions only), excluding HDB and cross-portal duplicates; (ii) they do not provide
per-prediction explanations accessible to end users; and (iii) they do not implement a
natural language interface. The URA REALIS transacted price dataset~\cite{ura2024realis} used
by Bian et al.\ is a public resource that could extend the present model to transaction-price
training in future work.

\section{Web Scraping as a Data Collection Methodology}

Landers et al.~\cite{landers2016inconvenient} provide a methodological primer on theory-driven
web scraping, arguing that automated data collection is appropriate when no public API or
licensed data source covers the target population and the scraped data is publicly accessible.
This is directly related to the current project: PropertyGuru does not have a public API, and any anonymous visitor can see all of the scraped attributes (price, floor area, tenure, district).
The PropertyGuru scraper uses a 1-second inter-request delay and an incremental crawl design,
roughly in line with what Landers et al.\ describe as a minimal-footprint approach to
ethical scraping.

\section{Recommender Systems}

\subsection{Content-Based and Collaborative Filtering}

The recommender systems literature broadly partitions approaches into three families.
\textbf{Content-based filtering} builds a user profile from attributes of items the user
has positively interacted with, then ranks unseen items by similarity to that
profile~\cite{ricci2011introduction}. It is fully cold-startable for new items and
requires no cross-user data, making it the natural baseline for platforms with a small
but growing user base. Its main limitation is the \textit{filter bubble}: users are
unlikely to be recommended items that differ from their interaction history in any
dimension.

\textbf{Collaborative filtering (CF)} instead exploits the co-interaction matrix: users
who liked the same items in the past are expected to share preferences for unseen
items. Koren, Bell, and Volinsky~\cite{koren2009matrix} demonstrated that matrix
factorisation (MF) approaches dominate neighbourhood-based CF on the Netflix Prize
benchmark, learning low-dimensional latent representations of users and items that
generalise well to unseen pairs. He et al.~\cite{he2017neural} extended MF with a
Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) framework that replaces the inner-product
interaction function with a multi-layer perceptron, improving accuracy on implicit
feedback tasks where only positive interactions are observed (clicks, purchases,
saves). Pure CF suffers from \textit{cold start}: new users or items with no
interaction history cannot be represented in the latent space.

\subsection{Hybrid Recommender Systems}

Burke~\cite{burke2002hybrid} provided a taxonomy of hybrid RS designs, identifying seven
combination strategies ranging from \textit{weighted} (linear combination of component
scores) to \textit{cascade} (one model filters, another re-ranks). The most common
production design is weighted hybridisation, where a content-based score and a CF score
are combined via learnable weights. A key finding from Burke's survey is that hybrid
systems consistently outperform either component in isolation, particularly at the cold-start
boundary where CF degrades but content-based methods remain stable.

The present system adopts the weighted hybrid design because the dataset is at the
cold-start stage for CF: fewer than ten registered users with sparse interaction histories
make latent-factor estimation unreliable. The content-based component is therefore the
primary ranker, while the \textit{bargain score}---derived from the XGBoost valuation
models---acts as a valuation-grounded auxiliary signal that encodes market-efficiency
information unavailable to a pure content-based approach.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics: NDCG}

Järvelin and Kekäläinen~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated} introduced \textbf{Normalised
Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)}, which extends binary relevance to graded relevance
labels and discounts gains logarithmically by rank position. Formally, for a ranking of
$K$ items:
\[
\text{NDCG}@K = \frac{\text{DCG}@K}{\text{IDCG}@K}, \quad
\text{DCG}@K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{2^{r_i} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)}
\]
where $r_i$ is the relevance label of the item at rank $i$ and IDCG is the DCG of the
ideal (oracle) ranking. NDCG has become the standard ranking metric in information
retrieval and recommender systems evaluation~\cite{ricci2011introduction}, replacing
binary Precision@K in settings where partial relevance is meaningful---precisely the
case here, where a listing matching the user's preferred property type but not district
(label~1) is a more useful result than a completely irrelevant listing (label~0), but
less useful than a full type-and-district match (label~2).

\section{Research Gaps and Positioning}

Previous studies have focused on many aspects of this issue individually. Nevertheless, there are still four unresolved issues related to these challenges in a Singaporean setting:

\textbf{Gap 1: Data integration across multiple portals.}
To date, there is no known publicly available database that accurately collects all available Singapore real estate data from Singapore’s four real estate data providers (PropertyGuru, 99.co, EdgeProp, and SRX) and eliminates duplicates, thereby integrating all into one dataset. Both Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} and Fan et al.~\cite{fan2006determinants} utilize an individual structured data source (i.e., URA REALIS or real estate agent records), which fails to capture the requisite quantity and timeliness of the real estate databases containing timely, real-time, multiple sources of data.

\textbf{Gap 2: Per-prediction SHAP explanations for end users.}
While Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} provide the theoretical foundation and
Jaouhari et al.~\cite{jaouhari2024mapping} identify SHAP as an emerging AVM standard, no prior
work specifically surfaces per-prediction SHAP factors to non-technical real estate consumers
through a production web interface in the Singapore context.

\textbf{Gap 3: LLM-based natural language search for Singapore property.}
Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024llm} demonstrate LLM-based structured information extraction from real estate text,
and Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020language} establish the few-shot capability that enables it.
However, no prior work applies LLM-based query-to-filter parsing to Singapore property search,
nor implements agentic fallback behaviours (multi-district resolution, progressive relaxation,
grounded chat) on top of such a pipeline.

\textbf{Gap 4: Valuation-grounded hybrid recommendation for Singapore property.}
Prior recommender systems in real estate adopt purely content-based or basic CF
approaches~\cite{ricci2011introduction} and do not integrate a machine-learned valuation
signal into the ranking function. No prior published work applies a hybrid ranking model
in which a per-segment AVM---trained on live multi-portal listing data---contributes a
\textit{bargain score} dimension that simultaneously ranks candidates by preference
alignment \emph{and} market-relative pricing efficiency within the Singapore context.

To the best of the author's knowledge, no prior published system simultaneously addresses all
four gaps within a single end-to-end deployable platform. This project bridges all four by
building an integrated system that unifies multi-portal data, surfaces SHAP-grounded per-property
valuations, enables conversational search with agentic fallback and a property-specific
chat assistant, and delivers valuation-grounded personalised recommendations evaluated
with NDCG on graded relevance labels.
