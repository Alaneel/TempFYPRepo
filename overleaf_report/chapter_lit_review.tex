\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Introduction}

Real estate valuation and market analysis sit at the intersection of economics, data science,
and artificial intelligence. This chapter surveys the evolution of methodologies in this domain,
ranging from classical econometric approaches to modern gradient-boosted machine learning and
large language model (LLM) applications. It also examines prior work on the Singapore housing
market—a uniquely dual-tier system—to contextualise the specific challenges and research gaps
addressed by this project.

\section{Traditional Real Estate Valuation: Hedonic Pricing Models}

The foundational framework for property valuation is the \textbf{Hedonic Pricing Model (HPM)},
introduced by Rosen~\cite{rosen1974hedonic}. This theory posits that a heterogeneous good such
as a house can be valued as the sum of its constituent attributes (e.g., location, size, number
of bedrooms), each commanding an implicit marginal price determined by equilibrium in the
product market.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical strengths.} HPMs provide interpretability and allow analysts to
    isolate the marginal contribution of individual features to price. The model directly
    motivates the feature engineering choices in this project—specifically, the use of
    \texttt{district}, \texttt{property\_age}, \texttt{sqft}, and \texttt{is\_freehold} as
    primary attributes.

    \item \textbf{Limitations.} Classical HPMs assume linearity and additive independence
    among regressors. Singapore's HDB resale market, where price discontinuities arise at
    \emph{lease eligibility thresholds} (60-year CPF rule, 30-year mortgage eligibility),
    cannot be adequately modelled under these assumptions~\cite{fan2006determinants}.
    Furthermore, HPMs cannot incorporate high-dimensional or non-linear spatial interactions
    without explicit feature construction.

    \item \textbf{Spatial extensions.} Can~\cite{can1992specification} extended the HPM
    framework to account for spatial autocorrelation—the observation that geographically
    proximate properties have correlated residuals. Can and Megbolugbe~\cite{can1997spatial}
    further demonstrated that ignoring spatial dependence produces biased hedonic coefficient
    estimates. This motivates the district-level geocoding pipeline in the present work:
    although a full spatial weights matrix is not implemented, the \texttt{district} feature
    serves as a proxy for first-order spatial stratification.
\end{itemize}

\section{Machine Learning for Automated Valuation}

\subsection{From Regression to Ensemble Methods}

The limitations of linear HPMs precipitated adoption of non-linear machine learning
techniques~\cite{park2015using}. Park and Bae~\cite{park2015using} demonstrated, on a
Fairfax County, Virginia housing dataset, that Random Forests and Neural Networks outperform
conventional OLS regression on held-out MAPE, particularly when feature interactions are
significant. Their work established the benchmark practice of reporting MAPE alongside
$R^2$, which this project adopts.

Fan, Ong, and Koh~\cite{fan2006determinants} applied CART-based decision trees to Singapore
private housing data, identifying location and project type as the dominant pricing splits.
While their dataset predates the post-2010 cooling measures and the use of more powerful
ensemble models, the variable importance findings directly corroborate this project's SHAP
analysis, in which \texttt{district} consistently ranks as the highest-magnitude feature
across all segments.

\subsection{Gradient Boosted Trees: XGBoost and LightGBM}

Chen and Guestrin~\cite{chen2016xgboost} introduced \textbf{XGBoost}, a scalable,
regularised gradient boosting framework that has since become the de facto benchmark on
tabular data tasks. Its use of second-order gradient statistics, column and row subsampling,
and combined L1/L2 regularisation makes it particularly robust to overfitting on the
moderate-sized per-segment datasets in this project ($n = 277$--$16{,}728$).

Ke et al.~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} subsequently proposed \textbf{LightGBM}, which improves
upon XGBoost through a leaf-wise (rather than level-wise) tree growth strategy and
Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS), achieving substantially higher throughput on
large datasets. In the present pipeline, both XGBoost and LightGBM are trained per segment;
XGBoost is ultimately preferred due to its marginally superior MAPE on the held-out test
sets across all eight segments, consistent with findings by Park and Bae~\cite{park2015using}
for US residential data and by Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} for Singapore private
properties.

\subsection{Automated Valuation Models at Scale}

Park and Bae~\cite{park2015using} evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms---including
decision trees, neural networks, and ensemble methods---for residential property price
prediction on a US county-level dataset, demonstrating lower MAPE than traditional hedonic
regression across multiple property types. This project corroborates their
finding for the Singapore context, with XGBoost reducing MAPE by 70.3\% relative to the
mean baseline on the Condominium Sale segment.

Oust, Hansen, and Pettrem~\cite{oust2023machine} took a different tack: instead of
choosing between repeat-sales indices and ML predictors, they stacked both, and the
hybrid beat either component alone on out-of-sample price index accuracy.
This project cannot replicate that approach---URA REALIS transacted prices are not
available to us (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations})---but the finding is relevant future
direction: if transaction data can be obtained, augmenting the current XGBoost models
with a repeat-sales index component is a well-motivated next step.

Jaouhari et al.~\cite{jaouhari2024mapping} conducted a systematic review of 89 AVM studies
published between 2010 and 2022, identifying that (i) gradient-boosted trees dominate recent
accuracy benchmarks, (ii) SHAP has become the leading tool for AVM interpretability, and
(iii) multi-source data integration is an emerging research frontier. This project contributes
to all three trends simultaneously: an XGBoost AVM with per-prediction SHAP explanations
trained on a unified multi-portal dataset.

\subsection{Model Interpretability: SHAP}

Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} introduced \textbf{SHAP (SHapley Additive
exPlanations)}, providing theoretically grounded, additive feature attributions derived from
cooperative game theory. SHAP values satisfy the axioms of local accuracy, missingness, and
consistency, making them more principled than earlier importance metrics (e.g., Gini impurity
or permutation importance). TreeExplainer—the exact SHAP algorithm for tree-based models—runs
in polynomial time and is applied in this project to produce the top-5 per-prediction SHAP
factors displayed in the user interface.

From a practical standpoint, Baur, Rosenfelder, and Lutz~\cite{baur2022automated} demonstrated
that incorporating NLP-derived text representations of listing descriptions alongside structured
attributes substantially improves AVM prediction accuracy, underscoring the value of unstructured
listing data as a complementary signal. This motivates the chat assistant component of the present
project: by grounding the assistant's responses in SHAP attribution data derived from the
structured feature set, the system makes the model's reasoning accessible to non-technical end
users in a way that goes beyond price alone.

\section{Large Language Models in Real Estate and Search}

\subsection{LLMs as Few-Shot Structured Decoders}

Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020language} established that large autoregressive language models
achieve strong performance on novel tasks via \textit{few-shot in-context learning}---without
any gradient updates---by conditioning on a small number of labelled examples in the prompt.
This capability underpins the natural language search pipeline in this project, where Claude
is prompted with eight few-shot query--filter pairs to parse free-text property queries into
structured JSON filters. A zero-shot version (no examples) was tested informally early on and
produced noticeably more district hallucinations, which is why the final prompt includes
the in-context examples.

Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024llm} demonstrated that transformer-based LLMs can extract
structured fields (price, floor area, tenure, party names) from free-text real estate
transaction contracts with high field-level F1, outperforming rule-based extractors on
edge cases such as abbreviated clauses or informal phrasing.
Their task differs from ours---they go from contract text to structured fields, whereas here
the input is a user query and the output is a search filter---but the underlying mechanism
is the same in both cases: an LLM maps unstructured text to a structured schema without
any task-specific fine-tuning.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} introduced \textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG)}, a framework in which a retrieval component surfaces relevant documents from an
external corpus, which are then prepended to the LLM's context before generation. The
valuation chat assistant in this project is architecturally a RAG-adjacent system: a
\textit{deterministic} retrieval step (database query → listing + valuation context) provides
the grounding context, and the LLM generates a conversational response conditioned on this
context. Unlike standard RAG, the retrieval here is exact (primary key lookup) rather than
approximate, which eliminates the hallucination risk associated with fuzzy document retrieval.

\subsection{Agentic LLM Pipelines}

Yao et al.~\cite{yao2022react} proposed \textbf{ReAct}, a prompting paradigm that interleaves
reasoning traces (\textit{Thought}) with environment actions (\textit{Act}) to solve multi-step
tasks. The progressive filter relaxation behaviour implemented in this project is structurally
analogous to a single-step ReAct loop: the LLM parses a query (Act), the system executes a
database query (Observe), and if no results are returned, a subsequent relaxation step is
triggered (Act again). This design choice—explicit agentic fallback rather than passive
zero-result deadends—is motivated by the user-facing recall failures documented in the
literature for conventional web search filters.

\section{The Singapore Housing Market}

\subsection{Dual-Tier Market Structure}

Singapore's residential market is uniquely bifurcated. The public sector, under the Housing and
Development Board (HDB), houses approximately 80\% of the resident population in a
government-subsidised leasehold system \cite{hdb2024annual}. Properties are sold on 99-year
leases, and resale eligibility is regulated by the Minimum Occupation Period (MOP) and CPF Board
rules tying loan eligibility to remaining lease duration. Tu~\cite{tu2004dynamics} analysed the
dynamics of the private housing market and identified significant mean-reverting behaviour in
price indices, while also documenting the regulatory constraint imposed by HDB policy on private
sector demand.

Sing, Tsai, and Chen~\cite{sing2006price} provided empirical evidence of price transmission
between the HDB resale and private condominium markets, demonstrating that rising private prices
pull up HDB resale prices through the ``asset progression'' mechanism. This inter-market
dependence motivates the present project's decision to train \textit{separate} models per
property type rather than a single cross-type model: the pricing dynamics of a two-room HDB
flat (government-regulated, narrow price band) are structurally incomparable to those of a
Good Class Bungalow (privately negotiated, $\geq$ S\$10M).

\subsection{Machine Learning on Singapore Property Data}

Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} applied both econometric and ML methods to Singapore
private property transaction data from URA REALIS, finding that Random Forest and XGBoost
outperform OLS hedonic regression on held-out MAPE. However, their work has three limitations
that the present project addresses: (i) they use a single-source dataset (URA REALIS private
transactions only), excluding HDB and cross-portal duplicates; (ii) they do not provide
per-prediction explanations accessible to end users; and (iii) they do not implement a
natural language interface. The URA REALIS transacted price dataset~\cite{ura2024realis} used
by Bian et al.\ is a public resource that could extend the present model to transaction-price
training in future work.

\section{Web Scraping as a Data Collection Methodology}

Landers et al.~\cite{landers2016inconvenient} provide a methodological primer on theory-driven
web scraping, arguing that automated data collection is appropriate when no public API or
licensed data source covers the target population and the scraped data is publicly accessible.
This directly applies to the present project: PropertyGuru does not offer a public API, and all
scraped attributes (price, floor area, tenure, district) are visible to any anonymous visitor.
The PropertyGuru scraper uses a 1-second inter-request delay and an incremental crawl design,
roughly in line with what Landers et al.\ describe as a minimal-footprint approach to
ethical scraping.

\section{Recommender Systems}

\subsection{Content-Based and Collaborative Filtering}

The recommender systems literature broadly partitions approaches into three families.
\textbf{Content-based filtering} builds a user profile from attributes of items the user
has positively interacted with, then ranks unseen items by similarity to that
profile~\cite{ricci2011introduction}. It is fully cold-startable for new items and
requires no cross-user data, making it the natural baseline for platforms with a small
but growing user base. Its main limitation is the \textit{filter bubble}: users are
unlikely to be recommended items that differ from their interaction history in any
dimension.

\textbf{Collaborative filtering (CF)} instead exploits the co-interaction matrix: users
who liked the same items in the past are expected to share preferences for unseen
items. Koren, Bell, and Volinsky~\cite{koren2009matrix} demonstrated that matrix
factorisation (MF) approaches dominate neighbourhood-based CF on the Netflix Prize
benchmark, learning low-dimensional latent representations of users and items that
generalise well to unseen pairs. He et al.~\cite{he2017neural} extended MF with a
Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) framework that replaces the inner-product
interaction function with a multi-layer perceptron, improving accuracy on implicit
feedback tasks where only positive interactions are observed (clicks, purchases,
saves). Pure CF suffers from \textit{cold start}: new users or items with no
interaction history cannot be represented in the latent space.

\subsection{Hybrid Recommender Systems}

Burke~\cite{burke2002hybrid} provided a taxonomy of hybrid RS designs, identifying seven
combination strategies ranging from \textit{weighted} (linear combination of component
scores) to \textit{cascade} (one model filters, another re-ranks). The most common
production design is weighted hybridisation, where a content-based score and a CF score
are combined via learnable weights. A key finding from Burke's survey is that hybrid
systems consistently outperform either component in isolation, particularly at the cold-start
boundary where CF degrades but content-based methods remain stable.

The present system adopts the weighted hybrid design because the dataset is at the
cold-start stage for CF: fewer than ten registered users with sparse interaction histories
make latent-factor estimation unreliable. The content-based component is therefore the
primary ranker, while the \textit{bargain score}---derived from the XGBoost valuation
models---acts as a valuation-grounded auxiliary signal that encodes market-efficiency
information unavailable to a pure content-based approach.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics: NDCG}

Järvelin and Kekäläinen~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated} introduced \textbf{Normalised
Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)}, which extends binary relevance to graded relevance
labels and discounts gains logarithmically by rank position. Formally, for a ranking of
$K$ items:
\[
\text{NDCG}@K = \frac{\text{DCG}@K}{\text{IDCG}@K}, \quad
\text{DCG}@K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{2^{r_i} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)}
\]
where $r_i$ is the relevance label of the item at rank $i$ and IDCG is the DCG of the
ideal (oracle) ranking. NDCG has become the standard ranking metric in information
retrieval and recommender systems evaluation~\cite{ricci2011introduction}, replacing
binary Precision@K in settings where partial relevance is meaningful---precisely the
case here, where a listing matching the user's preferred property type but not district
(label~1) is a more useful result than a completely irrelevant listing (label~0), but
less useful than a full type-and-district match (label~2).

\section{Research Gaps and Positioning}

Prior work addresses individual components of the present problem in isolation. Four specific
gaps remain unaddressed in the Singapore context.

\textbf{Gap 1: Multi-portal data integration.}
No prior published system aggregates and deduplicates property listings \textit{across all four
major Singapore portals} (PropertyGuru, 99.co, EdgeProp, SRX) into a single unified dataset.
Bian et al.~\cite{bian2020comparing} and Fan et al.~\cite{fan2006determinants} each rely on a
single structured data source (URA REALIS or agency records), which lacks the breadth and
recency of live multi-portal listing data.

\textbf{Gap 2: Per-prediction SHAP explanations for end users.}
While Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} provide the theoretical foundation and
Jaouhari et al.~\cite{jaouhari2024mapping} identify SHAP as an emerging AVM standard, no prior
work specifically surfaces per-prediction SHAP factors to non-technical real estate consumers
through a production web interface in the Singapore context.

\textbf{Gap 3: LLM-based natural language search for Singapore property.}
Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024llm} demonstrate LLM-based structured information extraction from real estate text,
and Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020language} establish the few-shot capability that enables it.
However, no prior work applies LLM-based query-to-filter parsing to Singapore property search,
nor implements agentic fallback behaviours (multi-district resolution, progressive relaxation,
grounded chat) on top of such a pipeline.

\textbf{Gap 4: Valuation-grounded hybrid recommendation for Singapore property.}
Prior recommender systems in real estate adopt purely content-based or basic CF
approaches~\cite{ricci2011introduction} and do not integrate a machine-learned valuation
signal into the ranking function. No prior published work applies a hybrid ranking model
in which a per-segment AVM---trained on live multi-portal listing data---contributes a
\textit{bargain score} dimension that simultaneously ranks candidates by preference
alignment \emph{and} market-relative pricing efficiency within the Singapore context.

To the best of the author's knowledge, no prior published system simultaneously addresses all
four gaps within a single end-to-end deployable platform. This project bridges all four by
building an integrated system that unifies multi-portal data, surfaces SHAP-grounded per-property
valuations, enables conversational search with agentic fallback and a property-specific
chat assistant, and delivers valuation-grounded personalised recommendations evaluated
with NDCG on graded relevance labels.
